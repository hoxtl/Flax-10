This chapter reflects on the management strategies employed throughout the project lifecycle.
The team adopted an Agile-inspired approach, utilising a Kanban workflow to manage the
transition from a client-led project to an academic one.

\section{Project Evolution}

The project initially began as a client-facing application for \textit{MakCorp}, a platform
managing data for over 800 ASX-listed companies, with the team tasked with building a data
warehouse focused on analysing mining stocks. At the start of week three, after careful
consideration, the team collectively decided to pivot to an academic project: \textbf{StudyFlax},
a time management system for King's College London students and teaching staff.

The decision was not taken lightly. The team recognised the value of working with a live
production system and appreciated the willingness of MakCorp to collaborate with students.
However, given the 10-week delivery timeline and the module's emphasis on structured
development, testing, and version control, the prevailing uncertainty around system access,
development environments, and technical architecture introduced risks that could not be
confidently managed within the academic constraints of the project. The pivot was therefore
a matter of aligning project scope with what the team could deliver reliably within the
semester, rather than a reflection of MakCorp's engagement or the quality of the opportunity.

To mitigate the risk of reduced development time following this switch, the team increased
its target goal to 18 user stories per week. This allowed the team to regain momentum and
ensure all core features of StudyFlax were delivered on schedule.

\section{Communication and Collaboration}

The team's communication strategy relied on a combination of synchronous and asynchronous tools:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Meetings}: The team first decided to hold two formal meetings per week
          (Mondays at 3:00~PM and Fridays at 2:00~PM) to discuss progress and blockers. However, due to unforeseen commitments from team members, we decided to schedule team meetings 2 days before each meeting to accommodate those who had flexible schedules.
    \item \textbf{Instant Messaging}: WhatsApp was used for daily coordination,
          with a protocol requiring members to reply within the same day.
    \item \textbf{Task Tracking}: A Trello Kanban board was used to visualise the backlog,
          in-progress tasks, and completed features
          (see team feedback, Trello Kanban board).
\end{itemize}

\section{Software Quality and Standards}

To maintain a high standard of software engineering, the team enforced strict coding conventions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Code Reviews}: All pull requests required approval from at least three team
          members before merging into the main branch.
    \item \textbf{Function Length}: Methods were limited to a maximum of 20-25 lines to ensure
          readability and low coupling.
    \item \textbf{Testing}: The team aimed for 100\% code coverage, with testing methods
          capped at 25 lines.
\end{itemize}

\section{Reflection and Lessons Learned}

The most significant lesson learnt was the importance of early risk identification.
Although the initial communication issues with MakCorp were a setback, communication within
the team and a clear division of responsibilities ensured that the team remained cohesive during
the transition. The use of a decentralised leadership structure allowed the team to move
faster than a traditional hierarchy would have allowed. For future projects, the team would
establish a more robust contingency plan for client-side delays earlier in the process.
