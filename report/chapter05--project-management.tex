This chapter reflects on the management strategies employed throughout the project lifecycle.
The team adopted an Agile-inspired approach, utilising a Kanban workflow to manage the
transition from a client-led project to an academic one.

\section{Project Evolution}

The project initially began as a client-facing application for \textit{MakCorp}, a platform
managing data for over 800 ASX-listed companies, with the team tasked with building a data
warehouse focused on analysing mining stocks. At the start of week three, after careful
consideration, the team collectively decided to pivot to an academic project: \textbf{StudyFlax},
a time management system for King's College London students and teaching staff.

The decision was not taken lightly with the team recognising the value of working with a live
production system and appreciated the willingness of MakCorp to collaborate with students.
However, given the 10-week delivery timeline and the module's emphasis on structured
development, testing, and version control, the prevailing uncertainty around system access,
development environments, and technical architecture introduced risks that could not be
confidently managed within the academic constraints of the project. The pivot was therefore
a matter of aligning project scope with what the team could deliver reliably within the
semester, rather than a reflection of MakCorp's engagement or the quality of the opportunity.

To mitigate the risk of reduced development time following this switch, the team increased
its target goal to 18 user stories per week. This allowed the team to regain momentum and
ensure all core features of StudyFlax were delivered on schedule.

\section{Communication and Collaboration}

The team's communication strategy relied on a combination of synchronous and asynchronous tools:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Meetings}: The team held two formal meetings per week was
(Mondays at 3:00PM and Fridays at 2:00PM) to discuss progress and obstacles. This was done for every week except for the one commencing on the 9th due to a variety of reasons: a lot of members had personal commitments during reading week and others caught the flu going around at the time which would have led to very poor attendance. A collective decision to slow down, review and polish our previous work during this week helped improve the code quality and clear up technical debts and missing tests at a critical moment in the project’s development being around the halfway point.

\item \textbf{Instant Messaging}: WhatsApp was used for daily coordination to discuss features within the team, ask for help and or otherwise communicate about the project and its progress. We used a mix of in person and online meetings with meeting notes, feature discussion, role allocation, progress updates and more being communicated regularly on WhatsApp as well as being documented on a shared Google Drive.

\item \textbf{Task Tracking}: A Trello Kanban board was used to plan the project and keep track of various features and their development. The trello board included two lists: a final submission checklist and a todo list for admin as reminders for ourselves. For the features it also included the backlog, in progress, ready for inspection, under review after inspection, ready for integration and completed lists. To expand on their purpose a bit further, the backlog was a way for us to collectively track the best ideas and any prospective features we discussed during meeting or via WhatsApp so that we could be aware of what features needed to be tackled next, who they might be assigned to, how we could label and group them and finally how they fit within the cohesive design of the website as a whole. In progress was a way to catalogue who was working on what, ready for inspection, under ready after inspection, ready for integration and completed to track the progress of the task. Finally labels were used and people assigned to tasks to visually categorise the tasks based on their functionality or design area and track the work of each team member.

\end{itemize}

\section{Software Quality and Standards}

To maintain a high standard of software engineering, the team enforced strict coding conventions:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Code Reviews}: All pull requests required approval from at least three team
      	members before merging into the main branch.
	\item \textbf{Function Length}: Methods were limited to a maximum of 15 lines to ensure
      	readability and low coupling.
	\item \textbf{Testing}: The team aimed for 100\% code coverage, with testing methods
      	capped at 25 lines.
\end{itemize}

\section{Workflow and Deployment}

As mentioned previously an agile inspired approach was used to manage the team’s workflow, quickly building up a prototype and expanding it with new ideas leading to a lot more experimentation and trialing for both UI and features early on. This allowed us to iterate and build up a minimum viable product (MVP) quickly to make up for the lost time due to the sudden switch in project type.

Github alongside Git was used to track all the team member’s contributions with each team member working on multiple features that each had their own separate branches. Within each branch we could then prototype the new features or UI to develop something we liked before adding it to the main version of the project. More specifically pull requests were created once we were happy with a feature which would then be reviewed by up to 3 people before being merged into main. Feedback was provided in this way to make the project more robust, ensure any changes were well tested and that they met a high standard of polish. The regular weekly meetings also allowed us to review how each feature progressed as team members discussed pull requests they reviewed or changes they made.

Deployment was also a huge focus for our group reflected by the regular deployment of the website on railway every one to two weeks. This decision was made to review how the site would appear in a deployment environment and catch any issues due to a change from production to deployment early. This proved to be a good decision as solving issues caused by API keys for both the Unimap and AI features took a significant amount of time and benefitted from the longer time allowing us to find the most reliable solution to them.

\section{Reflection and Lessons Learned}

The most significant lesson learnt was the importance of early risk identification.
Whilst the initial communication issues with Makcorp were a setback, communication within
the team and a clear division of responsibilities ensured the team remained cohesive during
the transition. The use of a decentralised leadership structure allowed the team to move
faster than a traditional hierarchy would have permitted. For future projects, the team would
establish a more robust contingency plan for client-side delays earlier in the process.
